  1. Crops and soils

    Genome-wide gene family scans with controlled stress expression assays in five crops

    Five new genome-wide surveys in maize, soybean, peanut, rice and tomato nominate stress-responsive genes, but only one tests function, in soybean hairy roots.

    Editorial correction

    The soybean study directly tested a gene’s role in alkaline tolerance. The rice study also went beyond measuring gene expression by checking where one protein was located inside cells. These experiments answered different questions. Soybean stress study · Rice localization study

    5 supporting papers

    The common design pairs in silico genome-wide identification with expression profiling under controlled stress. None of the studies includes field trials or yield data. In maize, 12 ZmARR-B genes were identified. Inbred lines 082, B73 and Ye107 were chosen from a leaf-Pi screen of 70 southwestern Chinese lines. Under short-term (0.5–12 h) low-P treatment, ZmARR-B03 was transiently induced within 0–6 h. ZmARR-B06 stayed upregulated through 14 d. More family members responded in P-efficient 082 than in P-sensitive Ye107. In peanut, reanalysis of public RNA-seq data mapped 71 circadian clock genes. Homologs of XAP5, PIF3, GI, FT and AGL15 were upregulated by Al in sensitive ZH2 but downregulated in tolerant 99-1507. In rice (19 OsDMP genes) and tomato (95 DnaJ/HSP40 genes), qRT-PCR confirmed responses to drought, salt, heat or hormones. Only soybean moved beyond expression: GmOXS3-1, one of 19 GmOXS3 genes, negatively regulated alkaline tolerance in hairy roots. The other genes remain correlative candidates.

  2. Crops and soils

    Pot, field and basket trials test amendments and cropping to curb cadmium in edible crops

    New studies on Cd-affected soils compare amendments, MnSO4 timing and cropping patterns for wheat, potato, maize and morel Cd against food safety limits.

    4 supporting papers

    The shared aim is limiting Cd transfer into edible tissues, with effects reported against untreated controls or monocultures. In alkaline-soil wheat pot experiments, thiol-modified attapulgite with low- or high-dose MnSO4 lowered grain Cd from 0.19 to 0.08 and 0.06 mg kg−1. Jointing-stage MnSO4 gave the lowest grain Cd. In field plots on high-geological-Cd karst farmland in Chongqing, maize–potato intercropping at 1:1 and 2:2 row ratios reduced fresh tuber Cd by about 38% versus potato monoculture, meeting the national limit. A field maize–wheat rotation combining wood vinegar with high-density planting raised maize stem and leaf Cd enrichment, while following-wheat grain Cd fell by up to 25.6% but stayed above the limit. In incubation and basket cultivation of Morchella sextelata (three replicates), LDH–biochar composites acted metal-specifically, cutting fruiting-body As by 53.9% (LB1) or Cd by 40.0% (LB2). Soil-fraction and microbial links are correlational.

  3. Crops and soils

    Field and pot trials tie split timing and moderate N rates to cereal N efficiency

    New wheat and maize field trials in China and a rice pot study show split N timing or mid-range rates raise N efficiency without yield loss.

    4 supporting papers

    All four studies treat N timing or N rate as a lever to decouple cereal yield from fertilizer input. In a two-year North China Plain field trial, subsoiling at 180 kg N ha−1 gave winter wheat yields of 8129.64 and 7700.89 kg ha−1. These did not differ significantly from 240 kg N ha−1, and PFPN reached 45.16 and 42.78 kg kg−1. In a two-year Northeast China black-soil maize trial, 180 kg N ha−1 yielded 4.1% less than 240 kg N ha−1. It cut N2O efflux by 25.5% and 0–80 cm soil mineral N residue by 17.0% relative to that rate. In a wheat fertigation experiment of unstated setting, a 5:3:2 jointing–anthesis–12 DAA micro-sprinkler split gave 10,578.6 kg ha−1 (+9.6% average) and better baking quality versus border irrigation. A two-season greenhouse pot study of rice cv. RD49 found the lowest urea rate matched the highest rate for grain weight, with basal-plus-topdressing outperforming basal-only. The pot results are explicitly framed as requiring field validation.

  4. Crops and soils

    Field and greenhouse trials: bacterial inoculant gains depend on N, P and soil context

    New trials test rhizobia and PGPB in bean, soybean, wheat and maize. Yield gains were significant only in intercropped soybean field trials.

    4 supporting papers

    The dominant thread is agronomic testing of bacterial inoculants against uninoculated or fertilizer-rate comparators, with responses that depend on context. In field trials of maize–soybean and sorghum–soybean intercrops, Bradyrhizobium japonicum S36 and Sinorhizobium fredii S65 raised soybean grain yield relative to uninoculated controls. Gains were 23.8% and 18.6% in the maize system and 49.2% and 40.4% in the sorghum system. In a two-season field trial on winter wheat in south-western Poland at 70–130 kg N ha−1, Priestia sp. KR219 increased chlorophyll a under reduced N. Yield increases were numerical only, not significant. On a Brazilian Cerrado Oxisol in a two-year field trial, BiomaPhos® showed inoculation × P interactions for maize yield and P-resin. These occurred only in the newly established, low-P no-till system, and only in season two. In an Ecuadorian greenhouse trial on bean, the lowest Kosakonia cowanii dose maximized nodulation (227.67 nodules plant−1). Grain yield did not differ significantly among treatments.

  5. Crops and soils

    Field trials: manure and mineral fertilization reshape soil bacteria, ARGs and CNPS genes

    Field experiments in semi-arid maize, Ningxia drylands and a 32-year rapeseed trial link manure-mineral regimes to soil bacteria, ARGs and CNPS genes.

    3 supporting papers

    All three field experiments compare manure-based and mineral fertilization and use amplicon sequencing, with high-throughput qPCR in two of them. In a semi-arid maize field, organic fertilizer treatments (OM, CF+OM, FDI+OM) increased soil ARG diversity and abundance relative to mineral-only CF and FDI. Drip fertigation (FDI, FDI+OM) reduced ARG abundance relative to conventional CF, OM and CF+OM. FDI+OM gave the highest bacterial abundance and diversity. DOC was the primary correlate of community structure. Modelled pathways placed organic carbon effects on ARGs through bacterial abundance and MGEs. In a long-term dryland trial in Ningxia, chemical fertilizer plus cattle or sheep manure raised the Simpson index relative to chemical fertilizer alone. Soil organic matter (p = 0.002) and pH (p = 0.03) explained community variation. In a 32-year trial, rapeseed grain yield exceeded the unfertilized control by 303.9% under NPK, 108.7% under manure and 397.8% under NPKM. Functional-gene responses differed between bulk and rhizosphere soil. Yield was associated with rhizosphere N-fixation and organic-P-mineralization genes.

  6. Crops and soils

    Lightweight YOLO variants for orchard, greenhouse and field crop detection, tested on image datasets

    Three image-dataset studies modify YOLO11n or YOLOv8n for detection under occlusion and lighting variation, targeting harvesting and yield estimation.

    3 supporting papers

    All three items are controlled computer-vision experiments on image datasets, not field agronomic trials or farm surveys. Each modifies a compact YOLO detector: TRSNet (YOLO11n) for plum ripeness in orchards, an improved YOLOv8n for eggplant fruits and stems in greenhouses, and BiF-YOLO (YOLOv11n) for seed maize ears in Jiaozhou and Zhangye production fields. The shared problem is occlusion, illumination change and scale variation. For plum, TRSNet reached mAP50 of 92.3% on the PRD dataset with 2.18 M parameters, outperforming named baselines, and was tested across orchards, varieties and on a blueberry dataset, with Jetson Orin Nano deployment. The eggplant model reached 99.0% mAP@0.5, up 0.5 points over YOLOv8n. The maize abstract gives no metrics. All figures are dataset-specific detection scores, not yield or field outcomes.

  7. Crops and soils

    Lightweight deep-learning weed localization in maize, cotton and Angelica fields: image-based tests

    Three Agronomy papers report image-based weed localization models for targeted weeding, with edge-deployment tests but no weed-control outcomes.

    3 supporting papers

    All three studies are image-dataset evaluations of lightweight YOLO-type or one-stage models for weed perception in maize seedling fields, cotton fields and Angelica dahurica fields. None reports a weed-control, yield or herbicide-use outcome. In maize, a crop-exclusion two-stage method (MSDNet plus HSV/PCA) reached 93.4% mAP@0.5 (+8.7 points over baseline) and 90.1% stem detection within 15 pixels (9.50 mm). In cotton, GDMM-YOLO11 reached 89.76 ± 0.66% mask mAP@0.5 over three seeds; its operation-point method improved mask inclusion and boundary clearance versus the centroid baseline, but not localization error. In Angelica, the detector reached 95.2% mAP@0.5 on an augmented test set and 91.8% on independent field validation. Edge inference was reported on Jetson hardware in the cotton and Angelica studies (357.67 FPS model-only; 46.8 FPS).
