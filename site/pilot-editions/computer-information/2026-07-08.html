  1. Computer systems

    Real-time systems research treats resource-management latency itself as a bounded resource

    ECRTS 2026 work bounds the timing of memory reclamation, cache and bandwidth allocation, and container orchestration on edge, multicore and serverless platforms.

    Editorial correction

    Only BRUMM budgets the time to reclaim a process group’s memory. MPORA changes cache and memory-bandwidth allocations and measures how often tasks meet deadlines. Its uncertainty estimates have a probabilistic coverage guarantee. PREEMPT-FaaS measures orchestration and service latency under load. Its sixfold improvement is a comparison with the baseline controller on the tested workloads. Each team measured a different operation. BRUMM · MPORA · PREEMPT-FaaS

    3 supporting papers

    The dominant thread is making the latency of resource-management operations predictable under contention, rather than only limiting resource quantities. BRUMM adds a reclamation-latency quota object to the L4Re microkernel. It reports consistent overestimation of reclamation latency within the same order of magnitude as measured values. Kernel overhead ranges from 2.4% for simple system calls to 28% in synthetic worst cases. PREEMPT-FaaS applies fixed-priority preemptive scheduling to Kubernetes and Knative orchestration requests.

    Under high load, including RNN and video-analytics workloads, it reports up to ~6× lower orchestration times and up to ~2 s lower 95th-percentile service latency for critical instances. MPORA allocates last-level cache and memory bandwidth to multi-path real-time tasks. It uses receding-horizon optimization with weighted conformal prediction for uncertainty-aware deadline constraints, implemented as a Linux kernel module with microsecond-scale inference. On SPEC CPU, it improves schedulability and response times over existing methods.

    All three contributions are supported by empirical evaluations; none reports formal latency proofs.

  2. Computer systems

    Predicting LLM serving cost, power and latency before profiling deployment configurations

    Three preprints replace exhaustive benchmarking of LLM deployment choices with learned or analytical predictors of throughput, power and latency.

    3 supporting papers

    The shared question is how to choose GPU type, parallelism, precision or layout for LLM inference without exhaustively measuring every configuration. OmniPilot learns a conformally calibrated quantile cost model from 460 runs on A100/H100/H200 across four precisions, reporting 6.2% throughput MAPE and 95% top-1 accuracy, with an OOD abstention layer that flagged all five unsupported holdout points where error rose to 24–46%.

    WattGPU instead predicts mean power and inter-token latency from public model and GPU metadata alone; under leave-one-GPU-out and leave-one-LLM-out validation over 42 LLMs and 8 GPUs, it reduces error roughly 2–4× versus load-scaled TDP and roofline baselines. Floor First takes an analytical route, bounding decode latency with a five-resource [max, sum] interval; its H20 case study on a 671B MoE/MLA model shows TP16 KV-capacity-limited to ~70 concurrent 8K requests versus ~644 for EP16+DP-attention.

    These are empirical and analytical estimates, not proofs.
